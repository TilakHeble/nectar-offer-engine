04 · Uplift models: comparison, selection and test results¶

Models are trained by python -m nectar.models.train (every run tracked in MLflow). This notebook reads the saved results.

Protocol

  • 43,326 (member, offer) pairs from the randomised experiment, split by member: 50% train, 25% validation, 25% test.
  • Target: incremental gross profit of sending the offer. Uplift models predict it directly, and their decision rule is offer when predicted incremental profit > 0.
  • Baselines: random (blanket) targeting, and a response model P(buy | offered), the usual "likely buyer" approach. Both baselines offer to the top 20% of their score.
  • Selection uses only observed outcomes on validation: an inverse-propensity-weighted estimate of incremental profit. Ground truth is never used for training or selection.
  • Test reports the same observed estimate plus the simulator's ground truth: how much of the best possible profit each model captures.

Reading the result

  • The response model loses the most money, more than random targeting. It finds members who are likely to buy, who are the members who need the offer least (notebook 03).
  • All uplift models except the T-learner make money. The T-learner fits two separate noisy profit models and subtracts them, so their errors swamp the small uplift signal.
  • The causal forest ranks best against the truth. The X-learner (the champion) and the ensemble come next.

2. What a live test would show¶

Without ground truth, a real team sees only the observed curve: the estimated incremental profit when the top-k% by score receive offers, computed from treatment vs control outcomes within that group. The dashed line is the true curve of a perfect ranking.

3. How certain was the model choice?¶

Validation has about 10,800 pairs. The 95% intervals below resample whole members.

va = scores[scores.split == "valid"]
rows = []
for name in models:
    s = va[f"score__{name}"].to_numpy()

The intervals of the leading models overlap heavily, so choosing the X-learner was within noise. The causal forest's true value was in fact higher. This is the honest limit of a 25% validation split on a 4-week test. In production the fix is a larger or longer test, or a champion/challenger split, rather than a different choice made after looking at the test data.

te = te.assign(decile=pd.qcut(te[f"score__{champion}"].rank(method="first"), 10, labels=False))
def by_decile(g):
    tr, co = g[g.treatment == 1], g[g.treatment == 0]
    return pd.Series({
        "predicted": g[f"score__{champion}"].mean(),
        "observed": tr.gross_profit.mean() - co.gross_profit.mean(),
        "truth": g.gt_profit_uplift.mean(),
        "pairs": len(g),
    })
dec = te.groupby("decile").apply(by_decile)
dec.index = [f"D{i + 1}" for i in dec.index]

5. What the champion has learned¶

A shallow decision tree is fitted to the champion's predictions (a surrogate model) to turn them into readable rules. The R² shows how much of the champion's behaviour the rules explain.

Summary¶

Result (test set, ground truth)
Blanket offers (random 20%) Lose money
Response model ("likely buyers") Loses the most money: it targets members who buy anyway
T-learner About break-even: too noisy on a small, mostly-zero profit signal
S-, X-, DR-learners, ensemble Profitable, capturing 43–67% of the best possible profit
Causal forest Best ranking, about 77% of the best possible profit
Champion (X-learner) Chosen on validation without ground truth; about 60% of the best possible profit

Interview line: on a randomised test, uplift targeting turned a loss-making blanket offer programme profitable. The model I'd ship was chosen without ever seeing the ground truth, and I can show how uncertain that choice was.

Next (week 7): a budget-constrained optimiser that assigns offers using these scores, with a cap on discount spend and on offers per member.
